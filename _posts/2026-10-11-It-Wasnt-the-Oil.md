## It Wasn't the Oil: What Really Pushed Rates Up in 2026

On September 16 the Federal Reserve raised rates for the first time since 2023, nine months after its last cut. Since late February the 10-year Treasury yield has gone from 3.97% to **5.22%**, and the 30-year mortgage rate from 5.98% to 7.40%. Two explanations are going around. One says the Fed caught everyone off guard: the market was braced for cuts and got a hike. The other says oil did it: Brent went from $71 to $125 a barrel, oil means inflation, and inflation means higher yields.

Public data shows both stories are mostly wrong, and the way they're wrong is more useful than either story. The hike was fully priced by the time it came. Oil did matter, but not through inflation, the channel everyone assumes. What lifted long rates is a rise in **real** yields and in the **term premium**, and it happened in every major bond market at once.

## Story one: "nobody expected a hike"

In February that was true. Treasury bills priced about **-33 bp** of cuts over the following 6 to 12 months, more than one further quarter-point cut. Then oil spiked. By March 26 the same measure had turned positive, and it never went back. On the eve of the September meeting bills priced **+89 bp** of hikes 6–12 months ahead and +51 bp over the nearer 3–6 months. Read plainly, that is one hike with more to follow.

The decision-day trading agrees. The 2-year rose +7 bp on September 16 and moved -7 bp the next day, fully reversing. A true hawkish surprise doesn't reverse overnight. The Fed had also signalled it openly: the July statement recorded three dissents in favour of hiking *then*. The surprise was the turn in March, from cuts to hikes. By September, the only people surprised were the ones still quoting February's forecasts.

### Why the Fed hiked with core inflation easing

This is the odd part, and the strongest reason to take the oil story seriously. Core CPI rose in the spring but is back down to **2.4%** in August, below where it began the year. Headline CPI, which includes energy, jumped from 2.4% in February to a peak of 4.2% in May. Unemployment came *down*, from 4.5% to 4.2%.

The minutes, released October 7, make the reasoning explicit. Participants judged longer-term inflation expectations "consistent with the Committee's 2 percent objective". The worry was what happens next. "Many participants assessed that the longer energy prices remained elevated, the greater the risk that cost increases in certain sectors could lead to broader price pressures", and some noted that "after more than five years of inflation above 2 percent, elevated inflation rates could begin to affect inflation expectations and wage- and price-setting decisions."

So oil did cause the hike, in a way. Not because it raised measured inflation expectations; it didn't. The Fed acted pre-emptively, so that an energy shock in a strong economy wouldn't become a sixth year of above-target inflation. That distinction is exactly what the bond market shows.

## Story two: "oil is pushing yields up"

Split the 10-year yield into its two parts: the real yield (what a TIPS investor earns above inflation) and the breakeven (the inflation compensation the market demands). If oil were lifting yields through expected inflation, the breakeven would carry the move.

It doesn't. Of the +125 bp rise, **+115 bp is real yield**, 92% of the total. The breakeven moved +10 bp while oil rose +76%. Investors aren't demanding much more protection against inflation. They are demanding a higher real return.

The cleanest test is the spring. Between March 31 and June 30 Brent fell **-44%**. If oil drove yields, they should have fallen. Breakevens did fall (-6 bp), by roughly what oil's historical effect would suggest. The 10-year still *rose* +14 bp and the 2-year +35 bp, because real yields went up +20 bp.

The change is in real yields. For a decade, oil moved breakevens and left real yields alone (correlation -0.07). This year the correlation is 0.44. Oil doesn't change what a 10-year bond pays after inflation. What it changes is the Fed's likely response, and the risk of holding duration while that response is uncertain. Each oil spike in 2026 was read as "more Fed", not "more inflation". Even taken at face value, this year's sensitivity (5.9 bp of 10-year yield per 10% move in oil) accounts for about **33 bp** of the +125 bp rise. Oil is part of the story, not most of it.

## What it actually is

Two things are left once the oil and surprise stories are set aside.

**A higher expected path for the Fed, and a higher price for uncertainty.** The Fed Board's own term-structure model (Kim-Wright, latest Oct 2) splits the 10-year yield into the average short rate expected over ten years and a term premium. The term premium went from 0.46% to 1.08%. That is **+62 bp, about 50%** of the move. The other half is expected policy, consistent with bills that moved from pricing cuts to pricing hikes.

The minutes list the candidates themselves: "incoming data that pointed to a stronger economy, increased expectations for AI-related borrowing, and geopolitical developments." That list matches the data. A strong economy and heavy expected AI capital spending raise the real return capital can earn, which shows up in real yields. Geopolitics and a Fed that just turned make the path less certain, which shows up in the term premium. Equities up +14% and credit spreads *tighter* say the same thing: investors are pricing a stronger economy, not distress.

## What to watch

- **October 27–28 FOMC.** Bills still price another hike within a year. A pause with hawkish language would test how much of the term premium is uncertainty about the Fed.
- **Core PCE.** It has sat near 3.0% all year. The case for hiking was that energy could spread into core. If it doesn't, the Fed's own logic points to a pause.
- **Real yields, not oil.** The read that matters is whether the next oil move shows up in TIPS breakevens (an inflation scare) or real yields (more Fed). So far it has been the second.
- **Other countries' yields.** If Gilts, Bunds and JGBs keep moving with Treasuries, the driver is global and won't be settled by a US data print.

## Notes on the data

Everything here comes from public sources, cached by a personal rates dashboard and analysed in Python: Treasury yields, TIPS real yields, breakevens, Brent (EIA), CPI/PCE, the Kim-Wright term premium, equities and credit spreads from FRED; auction results from TreasuryDirect; Bund yields from the Bundesbank, Gilt zero-coupon yields from the Bank of England, JGBs from Japan's Ministry of Finance; FOMC statements and minutes from federalreserve.gov. Data through October 9, 2026; Brent through Oct 6.

Some limits. The bill-implied policy path is rough, about ±10 bp, next to fed funds futures. The term premium is a model estimate, not an observation. The 2026 correlations rest on 41 weekly observations. Correlation is not causation, and oil and yields respond to much of the same geopolitical news. Where a figure depends on an estimate, the note under it says so.
